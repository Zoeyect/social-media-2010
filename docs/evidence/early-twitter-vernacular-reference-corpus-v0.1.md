# Early Twitter Vernacular Reference Corpus v0.1

## 1. Purpose

This document is a reusable tone reference for newly written, fictional Twitter content in *SOCIAL MEDIA, 2010*. It calibrates ordinary seed Tweets, Mentions, Direct Messages, work/life fragments, link sharing, and late-night chatter.

It is not a collection of October 20, 2010 events and does not authorize copying historical Tweets into runtime content.

## 2. Evidence classification

**VERNACULAR-CROSSCHECK**

- supports broad vocabulary, rhythm, informality, and interaction patterns;
- does not establish that any fictional sentence was posted;
- cannot independently create `HISTORICAL READY` content;
- must remain separate from Tweet-level provenance audits;
- final fictional sentences remain `CURATED` even when their language features are period-supported.

## 3. Capitalization

Observed/period-supported tendencies:

- lowercase starts and all-lowercase short updates;
- conventional capitalization mixed with loose mobile typing;
- occasional isolated ALL CAPS for emphasis;
- handles retain their account spelling inside otherwise lowercase text;
- capitalization can change between posts by the same ordinary user.

CURATED style examples, not quotations:

- `still at work lol`
- `OMG finally home`
- `why am i awake`

Rule: variation should come from voice/context, not a mechanical lowercasing filter.

## 4. Punctuation

Useful patterns:

- no terminal punctuation on short fragments;
- repeated question or exclamation marks;
- ellipses for hesitation or trailing context;
- missing apostrophes in fast typing;
- occasional slash or parenthetical aside;
- simple emoticons such as `:)` or `:(`, used sparingly.

CURATED examples: `seriously??`, `finally home...`, `cant sleep`.

Do not apply every pattern to every user. Grammar variation is evidence of casual communication, not a costume.

## 5. Slang and abbreviation table

| Form | Classification for 2010-style CURATED writing | Guidance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `lol`, `omg`, `haha` / `ahaha` | COMMON / STRONG | Well supported by pre-2010 informal-language studies and the 2009 OUP Twitter corpus summary. Use as reactions/discourse markers, not only literal laughter. |
| `wtf`, `lmao` | COMMON / STRONG, stronger register | Period-supported; use selectively by plausible ordinary users. |
| `idk`, `brb`, `btw`, `ttyl` | COMMON / STRONG | Established pre-2010 IM/SMS shorthand transferable to casual Twitter/DM tone. |
| `u`, `ur`, `ppl`, `thx`, `pls` | COMMON / STRONG | OUP specifically found contraction/abbreviation prominent; `u` and `ur` ranked among frequent Twitter vocabulary. Avoid assigning this style to every voice. |
| `fml` | PERIOD-SUPPORTED | Appropriate as an occasional 2009/2010 catchphrase; final sentence remains CURATED. |
| `epic fail` | PERIOD-SUPPORTED | Appropriate sparingly; already separately documented for the project live layer. |
| `rn` | HOLD for this corpus | Earlier usage likely existed, but this audit did not establish enough target-context evidence to make it a default 2010 voice marker. Prefer `right now` or omit. |
| `YOLO` as common slang; modern `stan`, `mood`, `lowkey/highkey`, `no cap`, `slay` | REJECT for default 2010 voice | Later/common-modern connotations would pull the simulation forward in time. A separately proven period-specific use would require its own audit. |

## 6. @reply behavior

Leading `@username` was a user-created convention that Twitter formalized. Twitter's May 2008 explanation describes a leading-handle Tweet as directly addressing someone while remaining public, and notes that a followed author's reply could appear in the recipient's main timeline. In March 2009 Twitter expanded the dedicated view from leading replies to all Tweets containing the user's handle.

This supports frequent, low-context public conversation:

- `@{handle} did u send it`
- `@{handle} lol no way`
- `@{handle} where are u`

The templates are newly written. They are not copied source Tweets.

Authoring rule: a healthy ordinary timeline should include conversation fragments, not only polished standalone observations. Routing must still follow the project's separately audited reply/follow rules.

## 7. Topic clusters

These clusters reflect the present-tense, activity-oriented character found in the 2009 OUP corpus. OUP reported unusually frequent first words such as watching, trying, listening, reading, and eating.

| Cluster | CURATED style patterns | Restraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work | `still @ work`, `finally sent the file`, `client said yes lol` | Avoid LinkedIn polish and universal office identity. |
| School | `forgot about homework fml`, `test tomorrow omg` | Balance with adults and non-school posts. |
| TV/media | `watching tv`, `anyone watching {thing}` | Avoid invented program/history claims without content review. |
| Phone/tech | `my phone is dying`, `twitpic isnt working` | Most technology should remain mundane, not historical exposition. |
| Weather | `its freezing`, `why is it still raining` | Short ambient texture is enough. |
| Bored/late-night | `cant sleep`, `bored`, `anyone still awake` | Do not make every late-night Tweet narratively meaningful. |
| Friends/social | `where r u guys`, `call me`, `lol send me that` | Keep relationships low-specificity unless the narrative requires more. |

## 8. Typos and mobile imperfection

Allowed in moderation:

- missing apostrophes;
- doubled letters for emphasis;
- occasional omitted word;
- accidental-looking capitalization;
- short forms plausibly encouraged by phone entry.

Do not deliberately misspell every record or assign the same typo pattern to every identity. One or two imperfections in a cluster are usually more convincing than systematic degradation.

## 9. Links

Period-appropriate reference hosts:

- `bit.ly` — STRONG; the March 2010 retweet study explicitly compares it with TinyURL;
- `tinyurl.com` — STRONG;
- `twitpic.com` — PERIOD-SUPPORTED for image-link behavior;
- `yfrog.com` — HOLD in this corpus until a selected use receives source-specific verification.

Style pattern: short reaction + raw shortened URL. Do not add a modern rich preview card. Do not assume `t.co`-only presentation for October 2010.

## 10. Contextlessness

Early Twitter's short, present-oriented writing permits fragments whose surrounding event is invisible:

- `finally`
- `that was awkward`
- `not again`
- `home.`

This is a primary writing rule. Avoid giving every post setup, punchline, historical framing, and resolution. A plausible social stream includes posts that make complete sense only to their author or immediate friends.

## 12. Fictional Tweet writing rules

Prefer:

- many posts between 3 and 15 words;
- occasional longer updates within the 140-character limit;
- casual lowercase mixed with ordinary capitalization;
- incomplete context;
- direct replies and brief reactions;
- different habits for different identities;
- raw short links when a verified/curated target exists.

Avoid:

- making every user witty;
- polished micro-essays;
- identical lowercase/slang across the whole cast;
- explanatory references to “2010”;
- modern influencer/brand voice;
- automatic random sentence generation.

Template forms, never final hardcoded Tweets:

- direct reply: `@{handle} {reaction}`
- work: `still @ work. {reaction}`
- late night: `{sleep fragment}`
- link: `{reaction} {shortLink}`
- TV: `anyone watching {thing}`
- vague: `{short unexplained reaction}`
- light achievement: `finally got {thing} done :)`

## 13. Demographic balance

The corpus must support students, working adults, young professionals, ordinary social users, and light tech/media chatter. No single interpretation of the device owner should dominate.

Practical balance checks:

- do not let school and homework define the full network;
- do not let office/client posts turn the feed into professional media;
- mix direct social exchanges with solitary status fragments;
- include activities, weather, entertainment, phones, and low-context reactions;
- avoid gender, relationship, geography, or age assumptions unless explicitly designed elsewhere.

## 15. Rejected modern language

Default rejection list:

- `YOLO` used as established mass catchphrase;
- modern `stan` identity/fandom usage;
- `mood` as a standalone reaction label;
- modern `lowkey` / `highkey` framing;
- `no cap`;
- contemporary meme `slay`;
- quote-Tweet cards, rich link unfurls, reaction counters, and influencer calls-to-action.

These are temporal guardrails, not claims that a string had never appeared anywhere. Any exceptional use requires separate dated evidence and content review.
